How the numbers work

Every number on this site is counted from public records, and the bills, filings and committees behind the numbers link to their sources. The data and the code that produce it are public, and the site updates itself through the day.

The Fear Index

Each of the {{ d.fear_word }} fears is scored from 0 to 100 against the loudest fear on each channel. Legislation counts 40%: bills, rules and orders citing the fear since January 2025. Lobbying counts 30%: federal lobbying filings naming the fear in the past year. Public attention counts 30%, split evenly between news coverage and Wikipedia readership over the last 30 days. Each channel is log scaled to its leader, so a fear with a tenth of the leader's bills still registers rather than rounding to zero, and a fear with no Wikipedia article on file is scored on the channels it has. The raw counts behind every score are shown on its page and in the grid.

Bills, rules and orders

State and federal bills introduced since January 2025, plus federal rules and executive orders, kept when they are substantially about artificial intelligence, algorithms, automated decisions, synthetic media or AI data centers. They are found by searching Congress.gov and Open States, which between them cover Congress, all 50 states, DC and Puerto Rico, against a fixed list of subject terms. The searches run continuously rather than once, so this is a record being filled in, not a finished list. A bill carried over into a later session is counted once. Federal rules and orders come from the Federal Register and are kept only when AI appears in the title or abstract.

Fears and controls

Each measure is read for the fears it cites and the controls it would impose, against fixed definitions of each. A first pass proposes labels. A second pass can only confirm them: it has to back each one with a quote copied from the measure's title or summary, and code checks that the quote is there, word for word. A fear or control label with no supporting quote of at least four words does not exist on this site, and an office name needs two, since some are two words long. A quote from a summary's account of the law already in force does not count, and a legislature is not counted as an office. The code proves the quoted words are in the measure. It cannot prove they bear the label out, so a third reading, by a separate model, asks of every control and office label whether the measure itself does what the label says, rather than permitting it, loosening it or rewarding it, and takes off the labels that fail. A label that has not been through that reading cannot lead the daily post. A label found to misread its measure is also taken off by hand, with the reason written into the public code, and once a week the labels most likely to be wrong are listed for a person to read. Every stored label is re-checked against the current rules on every run.

In their own words

Each quote is the line a label rests on, copied from the measure's official summary: the Congressional Research Service's for a federal bill, the legislature's digest or abstract for a state bill, and the agency's abstract for a federal rule. The front page shows the five bills that would hand out the most: ranked by the number of controls the bill carries, whether it names an office, whether it has passed, and how much of the controlling language sits in the quoted line itself. Companion bills with the same text count once. Bill titles written in capitals and lines under six words are left out.

Who's funding it

Federal lobbying filings whose issue descriptions name one of the tracked fears, over the last four quarters whose reports are due. A client that lobbies in-house counts what it paid outside firms among its own expenses, so where a client files its own report, its firms' reports for that quarter add nothing to the dollars. Advocacy groups and foundations are ranked by dollars, because an organization whose stated purpose is a given risk is spending that money on the fear. Companies and trade groups are ranked by number of filings, because a trade group's filing reports one total for everything it lobbied on that quarter, so its dollar figure says how much it lobbies, not how much it spends on AI. Those dollar figures appear on each organization's page with that note attached.

Election money

Money raised by political committees working on AI policy, counted by committee and kept out of the funding ranking, because a committee's spending does not say which side of a fear it is on. The committees are found two ways: by name, for the ones already tracked here, and by searching the FEC's own register for committees whose names carry AI, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deepfake, algorithm or data center. A committee that has registered but raised nothing is left out until it has. Independent expenditures are reported separately{% if d.election_spent %}, and come to {{ d.election_spent }} so far{% else %}, and no committee tracked here has made one yet{% endif %}. Donors appear on the receiving committee's page, each linked to a filing.

Who benefits

Government agencies and officials that a measure carrying a control would hand new authority, jurisdiction or enforcement power over AI, counted by the number of those measures. Legislatures and bare jurisdiction names are excluded.

Public attention

Daily Wikipedia readership of each fear's articles, and daily news volume from GDELT. Headlines come from publishers' own feeds, and a story counts for a fear only when every word that fear is defined by appears in it. The polling lines at the top of the front page are from the surveys they link to, are dated, and leave the page at eighteen months. The one headline at the very top comes from one of the publishers listed under Headlines below, whose feeds this report reads in full, and it is the story of the leading fear that shares the most words with the rest of the week's. Where several report the same thing, it comes from the one writing about the most of the {{ d.fear_word }} fears. GDELT sweeps the whole web, which makes it a fair measure of how loud a fear is and a poor one of whom to quote.
