How the numbers work

Every number on this site is counted from public records, and every bill, filing and committee behind it links to its source. The data and the code are public, and the site updates itself through the day.

The Fear Index

Each of the {{ d.fear_word }} fears gets a score from 0 to 100. Legislation counts for 40%: the bills, resolutions, rules and orders since January 2025 that cite the fear. Lobbying counts for 30%: federal lobbying filings that name it in the past year. Public attention counts for the last 30%, split evenly between news coverage and Wikipedia readership over the past 30 days. On each channel a fear is measured against the leading fear on a log scale, so one with a tenth of the leader's bills still shows up. A fear with no Wikipedia article is scored on the channels it has. Each fear's page shows the raw counts behind its score.

What counts as a measure

State and federal bills and resolutions introduced since January 2025, or filed for a legislative session that began then, plus federal rules and executive orders. A measure counts when it is about AI, algorithms and automated decisions, deepfakes and other synthetic media, or data centers. Data center bills count whether or not they mention AI, because the data center boom is being driven by AI. A bill whose title names AI or data centers always counts. A bill that mentions AI only in passing, such as an agriculture bill that lists it among other technologies, does not.

Bills come from Open States and Congress.gov, which together cover Congress, all 50 states, DC and Puerto Rico. Rules and executive orders come from the Federal Register. The searches run every day, so the record is still being filled in. It is also checked against a list of AI laws whose outcomes are known from other sources: a missing law is looked up and added, and anything still wrong is flagged. A bill carried over into a later session counts once.

State by state

The map shades each state by its AI measures that would put AI under new government control, on a log scale against the state with the most, and can switch to what is already law or to one fear. A state's count also reflects how much its legislature files. Where the search that finds bills cannot see a legislature's bill text, its bills are read one at a time instead, and its square is dashed until that is done.

Passed and not passed

Where a measure stands comes from the last action its legislature recorded. Passed means signed, chaptered or otherwise enacted, including a law that took effect without the governor's signature. Sent to the governor (or the president, for a federal bill) means the legislature passed it and it has not been signed or vetoed yet. Did not pass means the record shows it stopped: voted down, vetoed, withdrawn or postponed indefinitely. Everything else is not passed. Most legislatures record nothing when a session ends, so the record alone cannot say whether a bill is still alive. A resolution that passed is adopted, a federal rule is proposed or final, and an executive order is signed.

Fears and controls

Each measure is labeled with the fears it cites and the controls it would impose, using a fixed definition for each. Every label rests on a quote copied word for word from the measure's official title or summary, and every label is checked against that text. Labels that don't hold up are removed. Only what the measure itself would do counts, not its description of laws already on the books. A measure with no official summary can only be labeled from its title. Once a week the labels most likely to be wrong are listed for review.

In their own words

The quotes on the front page are the lines the labels rest on, copied from each measure's official summary. The five shown are the bills that would hand out the most power, ranked by how many controls they carry, whether they name an office, and whether they have passed. Companion bills with the same text count once.

Who's funding it

Federal lobbying filings from the last four reported quarters whose issue descriptions name one of the fears. Advocacy groups and foundations are ranked by dollars. Companies and trade groups are ranked by number of filings, because each of their filings reports one total for everything they lobbied on that quarter, so the dollar figure would overstate what they spent on AI. Those dollar figures are on each organization's page with that note. A client that lobbies in-house already reports what it paid outside firms, so that money is not counted twice.

Election money

Money raised by political committees working on AI policy. They are found by name and by searching the FEC's register for committees with AI, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deepfake, algorithm or data center in their names. Committees that haven't raised anything are left out. This money is kept apart from the funding ranking, because a committee's spending doesn't show which side of a fear it is on. Independent expenditures are reported separately{% if d.election_spent %}, and come to {{ d.election_spent }} so far{% else %}, and no committee tracked here has made one yet{% endif %}. Donors appear on each committee's page, linked to their filings.

Who benefits

The government agencies and officials that measures carrying a control would give new authority or enforcement power over AI or data centers, ranked by how many of those measures name them. Legislatures are not counted.

Public attention

Wikipedia readership of each fear's articles and news volume from GDELT, over the past 30 days. Headlines come from the publishers' own feeds, and a story counts toward a fear only if it contains every term that defines the fear. The polling lines at the top of the front page link to their surveys, are dated, and come down after 18 months. The headline at the very top is a story about the leading fear, from one of the publishers listed below, picked as the one most like the rest of that week's coverage.
